# Course 2 · Unit 1.1 — Where AI helps from enquiry to renewal

## Slide-by-slide narration script (TTS-ready)

Editorial draft · 18 October 2026 · intended audience: agents, bancassurance officers and customer service teams.

Learning outcomes: map the journey; distinguish evidence from assumptions; assign preparation and decision ownership; check a customer reply; prepare a human handover. Target: approximately 9–11 minutes, to be measured after synthesis. Every person and product in the example is fictional. See `../C02_Reference_Map.md` for source roles.

### SLIDE 01 — A customer asks a simple question

**On-screen text:** “I use a motorcycle for deliveries. Can you explain what cover I might need? Please reply here.” · Fictional enquiry

**Visual:** A fictional message card beside an agent's unfinished task list. No real customer name or number.

NARRATION:
A customer messages a branch or an insurance agent. They use a motorcycle for deliveries and want to understand what cover they might need. They ask you to reply in the same conversation. It sounds like a simple question. But before you answer, you need to understand the customer's situation, find the relevant product information, explain it accurately and know when to refer. Artificial intelligence can help prepare that work. In this unit, we follow this fictional enquiry through the customer journey and see which tasks A-I can assist with, and which responsibilities remain with people.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 02 — What you will be able to do

**On-screen text:** Map the journey · Separate facts from guesses · Check a reply · Hand over with context

**Visual:** Four labelled checkpoints along a simple horizontal path.

NARRATION:
By the end, you should be able to do four things. First, describe the journey from an enquiry to ongoing service and renewal. Second, separate what the customer actually said from what a system has guessed. Third, check an A-I-prepared reply before a customer receives it. And fourth, prepare a handover that helps the next person act without making the customer repeat everything. The aim is practical: use A-I to improve preparation, while keeping each customer-facing action and each insurance decision with the right owner.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 03 — See the whole journey

**On-screen text:** Enquiry → Discovery → Explanation → Referral/application → Service → Renewal review

**Visual:** A six-step journey. Put a person or team under each step; mark “cover status must be evidenced” beneath application.

NARRATION:
Think of the journey as six stages. An enquiry arrives. Discovery clarifies the need and the facts still missing. Explanation helps the customer understand relevant information. Referral or application moves the case to the authorised process. Service handles questions and updates after that. A renewal review considers whether the customer's situation or the available terms have changed. Customers can move backwards or stop at any point. An application is not the same as confirmed cover. At each stage, record what has actually happened, what happens next and who is responsible.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 04 — Different channels, clear ownership

**On-screen text:** Agent · Branch · Insurer · Customer service · Same need, different handovers

**Visual:** Four role cards connected to one fictional enquiry record. Do not imply cross-organisation access is already configured.

NARRATION:
An enquiry may start with an agent, at a bank branch or in a service inbox. Several teams may help, but a customer should not have to work out the internal organisation chart. In a bank-and-insurer example, the branch prepares information and refers through its approved process. The authorised insurer team handles decisions within its role. Your own organisation must define these responsibilities. A-I can summarise the enquiry for the next person, but it cannot create authority that a person or system does not already have. A clear owner and next action matter more than a long automated conversation.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 05 — What is known, and what is missing?

**On-screen text:** Stated: motorcycle used for deliveries; wants an explanation; reply in this conversation · Unknown: applicable product, eligibility, terms, premium, existing cover

**Visual:** Two columns: “Customer stated” and “Still unknown”. An AI suggestion card stays outside both columns.

NARRATION:
Return to our fictional customer. We know that they use a motorcycle for deliveries, want an explanation and asked for a reply here. We do not know the applicable product, whether the use is eligible, the terms, the premium or whether they already have cover. A fluent system might fill these gaps with a plausible story. That would make the reply sound complete while weakening its accuracy. Keep unknowns visible. A customer statement is evidence of what they said. It is not proof that an insurer has accepted the risk or that a particular benefit applies.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 06 — Match the task to the tool

**On-screen text:** Rules: reminders and routing · Generative AI: drafts and summaries · Agentic workflow: prepares multiple steps within defined permissions

**Visual:** Three tool cards with a review checkpoint before any customer-facing action.

NARRATION:
Not every useful task needs a complex agent. A fixed rule can route a new enquiry to a branch queue. A generative assistant can draft a summary or rewrite an approved explanation in clearer language. An agentic workflow may combine several preparation steps, such as finding the relevant source, collecting unanswered questions and preparing a handover. These are examples of possible designs, not a promise that every function exists in your current system. Choose the simplest tool that helps with the task. Then define what it can read, what it may prepare and what needs a person before anything changes or is sent.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 07 — Ground the explanation in the right source

**On-screen text:** Approved source · Applicable version · Exact evidence · Unknowns referred

**Visual:** A fictional product extract labelled “Training extract — not an insurance offer”, linked to one sentence in a draft.

NARRATION:
For this exercise, imagine an approved training extract says that delivery use must be referred for confirmation and that terms depend on the insurer's review. That is the only product information we have. A useful draft must stay within it. It may explain that delivery use needs review and ask focused follow-up questions. It must not invent a premium, promise a claim payment or say that cover is already active. Save the source reference and its version with the draft so the reviewer can check the exact passage. If you cannot find support for a statement, remove it or refer the question.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 08 — Compare two replies

**On-screen text:** A: “You are covered for deliveries. All claims will be paid.” · B: “I can help explain the options. Delivery use needs confirmation. May I ask a few questions before we refer it?”

**Visual:** Two fictional draft cards. Highlight the unsupported guarantees in A; show “review before sending” on B.

NARRATION:
Compare two replies. Reply A says, you are covered for deliveries and all claims will be paid. It is short and confident, but neither statement is supported. Reply B says, I can help explain the options. Delivery use needs confirmation. May I ask a few questions before we refer it? Reply B moves the conversation forward while preserving uncertainty. It still needs review for the organisation's wording, the channel and the actual source. Clear communication is not a choice between sounding confident and saying nothing. You can give a useful next step without promising an outcome you cannot confirm.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 09 — Review before a customer-facing action

**On-screen text:** Right customer? · Supported wording? · Suitable channel and contact status? · Clear next step? · Authorised reviewer?

**Visual:** A five-item review checklist between a draft and a send arrow. Reviewer can edit, return, stop or refer.

NARRATION:
Before a prepared reply is sent, check five things. Is it for the right customer and enquiry? Does each product statement have supporting evidence? Is the channel appropriate for this contact and the organisation's current contact rules? Is the next step clear? And does the reviewer have authority to approve this action? Review should be a real opportunity to edit, return, stop or refer. A routine click that nobody has time to question adds little protection. If the wording is unsupported, correct it. If the source is missing, pause that claim. Do not turn the customer into the final quality checker.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 10 — A score helps organise work

**On-screen text:** Follow-up priority ≠ eligibility ≠ acceptance · Reasons and missing facts must stay visible

**Visual:** Two fictional enquiries with different next actions. Avoid invented real IIP scores or scoring bands.

NARRATION:
A team may use a score or a worklist to organise follow-up. Treat it as a prompt to inspect the evidence, not as a conclusion about the customer. A high-priority enquiry is not automatically eligible for cover. A low-priority enquiry is not a rejection. Ask what the priority is based on, which facts are missing and whether the next action is appropriate. For our customer, a useful next action may simply be to clarify delivery use and prepare a referral. The quality of that action matters more than making a number look precise.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 11 — Make the handover useful

**On-screen text:** Stated need · Known facts · Unknowns · Source/version · Contact status · Next action/owner

**Visual:** A short handover record with six labelled fields and a link back to the enquiry.

NARRATION:
A useful handover contains the customer's stated need, the known facts, the unanswered questions, the source used, the current contact status and the next action with an owner. Our fictional note might say: customer requests an explanation for a motorcycle used in deliveries; eligibility and premium are unconfirmed; training extract requires referral; reply requested in this conversation; assigned officer to clarify the required details and refer. Keep irrelevant personal details out. The purpose is to help the next person verify and act. A long transcript alone is not a handover, and a summary without its supporting record is harder to check.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 12 — Handle service, renewal and stop requests

**On-screen text:** Explain what is known · State what remains pending · Route disputes · Honour the recorded stop condition

**Visual:** Three branches from the journey: service question, renewal review and stop/escalate.

NARRATION:
The same preparation discipline applies after an application. A service update should distinguish a recorded status from an expected outcome. A renewal conversation should check current terms and changed needs rather than assuming the previous arrangement continues. If a customer disputes a statement or asks for a person, route the issue with context. If they ask you to stop outreach, record that request and stop the relevant workflow under the organisation's procedure. Do not keep sending because the next message was already drafted. A-I can prepare a helpful update, but the workflow must respect the latest evidence and contact status.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 13 — Measure usefulness, not just activity

**On-screen text:** Response time · Complete handovers · Corrections · Complaints · Stop requests honoured · Commercial outcomes with a denominator

**Visual:** A fictional measurement sheet with column headings only; no invented results or improvement percentages.

NARRATION:
More messages do not automatically mean a better customer experience. Look at response time, whether handovers contain the required information, how often drafts need material correction and whether concerns or stop requests are handled properly. Commercial measures can be useful too, but define the population, period and denominator. Ten completed applications means something different out of twenty qualified enquiries than out of two thousand contacts. Compare like with like and check the quality of the outcome. For a small pilot, choose a few measures that the team can actually record and review before expanding the workflow.
END NARRATION

### SLIDE 14 — Apply the checks, then take the quiz

**On-screen text:** Facts before guesses · Sources before claims · Review before action · Owner at every handover · Ten-question quiz

**Visual:** The journey returns with four review markers. The quiz screen shows no AI-assistant bubble.

NARRATION:
Let us bring it together. Map the customer journey, keep facts separate from assumptions, ground product statements in the applicable source and assign the next action to the right person. Before you send, check the customer, wording, contact status, next step and authority. For your practice task, write a three-sentence reply to the fictional motorcycle enquiry and a short referral note. Use only the training facts supplied in this unit. Then take the ten-question quiz independently. The assessment is designed to hide the A-I assistant. In the next unit, we develop the discovery questions that help you understand the need without guessing.
END NARRATION
